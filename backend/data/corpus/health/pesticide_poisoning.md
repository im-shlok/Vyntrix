# Pesticide Poisoning First Aid

Pesticide poisoning on farms happens through the skin while spraying, by
breathing spray mist, and by swallowing, either accidentally or because
pesticide was stored in a food or drink container. Organophosphate and
carbamate insecticides such as chlorpyrifos, profenofos and quinalphos cause
most serious cases. Hospitals treat them with atropine, which must only be
given by trained medical staff.

## Symptoms
- Headache, dizziness, blurred vision or very small (pinpoint) pupils
- Heavy sweating, drooling, watery eyes or a runny nose
- Nausea, vomiting, stomach cramps or diarrhoea
- Muscle twitching, weakness, a tight chest or difficulty breathing
- Confusion, fits or unconsciousness in severe cases

## First aid steps
1. Move the person out of the sprayed area into fresh air, wearing gloves so that you are not contaminated too.
2. Remove contaminated clothing and wash the skin and hair thoroughly with soap and plenty of clean water.
3. If pesticide entered the eyes, rinse them gently with clean running water for at least 15 minutes.
4. If it was swallowed and the person is conscious, rinse the mouth with water. Do NOT induce vomiting unless the product label says to.
5. If the person is drowsy or vomiting, lay them on their side so they do not choke, and keep them warm.
6. Call 108 and take the person to the nearest primary health centre or hospital with the pesticide container or label.

## Do not
- Do not give milk, oil, salt water or home remedies. They do not neutralise pesticide and can cause choking.
- Do not give anything by mouth to a person who is unconscious or having fits.
- Do not wait for symptoms to get worse. Organophosphate poisoning can stop breathing within hours.

## Why the label matters
The doctor's treatment depends on the chemical group, so always carry the
label or container with the patient. Some herbicides, such as paraquat, have
no antidote and can be fatal even when the person seems well at first, so
every case of swallowed pesticide needs hospital care.
