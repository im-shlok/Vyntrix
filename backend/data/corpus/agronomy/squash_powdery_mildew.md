# Squash Powdery Mildew Leaf Management

Powdery mildew on squash and cucurbits is primarily caused by the obligate biotrophic fungus *Podosphaera xanthii* (syn. *Sphaerotheca fuliginea*) or *Golovinomyces cichoracearum* (syn. *Erysiphe cichoracearum*). It is one of the most widespread foliar diseases affecting zucchini, pumpkin, winter squash, and cucumber, thriving in warm, dry weather (20°C to 28°C) accompanied by shaded conditions and high relative humidity without direct rainfall.

## Identification

Symptoms begin as small, talcum-powder-like, circular white spots on the undersides of older leaves, lower crown foliage, and leaf petioles where shade and humidity are highest. As the disease progresses, these spots expand rapidly and coalesce, covering both the upper and lower surfaces of leaves in a dense, white, powdery talc-like mycelial felt. Heavily infected leaves lose chlorophyll, turn yellow, become brittle and brown, and die prematurely. Loss of canopy foliage exposes developing squash fruit to severe sunscald, premature ripening, and diminished sugar concentration.

## Distinguishing Powdery Mildew from Downy Mildew

Powdery mildew produces superficial white, powdery fungal growth primarily on the upper and lower leaf surfaces that can be wiped off with a finger. Downy mildew (*Pseudoperonospora cubensis*) produces angular, bright yellow lesions strictly delimited by leaf veins on the upper surface, with a purplish-gray downy mold on the corresponding lower surface only during moist mornings.

## Cultural control

Plant squash varieties with genetic resistance to powdery mildew whenever available (many modern F1 hybrids carry multi-race resistance). Space squash hills or rows generously (1.5 to 2 metres apart) to maximize sunlight penetration and airflow through the canopy, as direct UV radiation inhibits powdery mildew spore germination. Avoid excessive nitrogen applications, which produce soft, succulent foliage highly prone to rapid fungal colonization. Remove and destroy heavily infected older leaves early in the cycle.

## Organic management

Organic suppressive sprays are highly effective if applied at the first sign of white spots. Spray potassium bicarbonate (3 to 4 g per litre) with an organic sticker. Horticultural mineral oils or neem oil (5 ml per litre) suffocate the fungal mycelium and prevent spore germination. Diluted cow's milk spray (10% to 20% solution in water) exposed to sunlight produces free radicals that disrupt fungal hyphae. Wettable sulfur (2 to 3 g per litre) is effective; do not apply sulfur when temperatures exceed 32°C to avoid severe foliar phytotoxicity.

## Chemical management

Preventive and early curative systemic fungicides should be alternated to combat resistance. Effective options include myclobutanil, tebuconazole (1 ml per litre), azoxystrobin (1 ml per litre), penconazole, or cyflufenamid. Never use the same chemical class more than twice consecutively. Always adhere to product pre-harvest intervals and consult your local Krishi Vigyan Kendra for approved regional cucurbit recommendations.
