# Grape Leaf Black Rot (Guignardia bidwellii) Management

Black rot of grape is caused by the ascomycete fungus *Guignardia bidwellii* (anamorph *Phyllosticta ampelicida*). It is one of the most destructive diseases of cultivated grapevines (*Vitis vinifera* and hybrids) in warm, humid regions, causing significant foliar leaf spotting, petiole girdling, and complete mummification of berry clusters.

## Identification

Foliar symptoms appear in late spring on young, actively expanding grape leaves as tiny, circular, cream-coloured to reddish-brown necrotic spots. As the lesions expand (typically 2 to 10 mm in diameter), they develop a distinct dark reddish-brown border surrounded by an irregular chlorotic halo. The hallmark diagnostic feature of black rot on grape leaves is the appearance of tiny, raised, black pinhead-like pimples (pycnidia) arranged in a neat ring or circle just inside the outer margin of the reddish-brown spot. Severe foliar infection leads to petiole and shoot cankers, which girdle and kill entire leaf shoots. Later in the season, infected berries shrivel into hard, black, wrinkled "mummies" that cling to the vine.

## Distinguishing Black Rot from Anthracnose and Downy Mildew

Black rot foliar spots have a tan centre, a dark reddish-brown margin, and a characteristic ring of black pycnidia pimples inside the margin. Grape anthracnose (*Elsinoë ampelina*) produces small sunken spots with grey centres and prominent dark purple-black raised margins ("bird's-eye" rot) where the centres commonly drop out to form shot holes. Downy mildew (*Plasmopara viticola*) produces yellowish oily "oil-spots" on the upper leaf surface with a white, downy cottony sporulation on the underside in humid conditions.

## Cultural control

Sanitation is the most critical cultural control:
1. Mummy eradication: Prune away and destroy all shrivelled, mummified grape clusters and infected canes during winter dormant pruning. Any mummies left on the trellis wire or vineyard floor serve as the primary source of ascospores during spring rains.
2. Canopy management: Practice canopy shoot positioning, suckering, and leaf pulling around the fruiting zone during early post-bloom to maximize sunlight penetration and air movement, which ensures grape leaves dry within a few hours after rain.
3. Vineyard floor sanitation: Plough under or cultivate vineyard floor debris before bud break to bury fallen infected leaves beneath the soil.

## Organic Management

Apply preventive copper-based sprays (copper hydroxide or Bordeaux mixture 1% at 10 g per litre) or wettable sulfur (3 to 5 g per litre) starting at 2 to 3 cm shoot growth through early post-bloom. Ensure sprays precede rainfall events, as infection occurs within 6 to 9 hours of continuous leaf wetness between 20°C and 27°C.

## Chemical Management

Protecting young grape foliage and clusters from 10 cm shoot growth through 4 weeks post-bloom is critical. Effective fungicides include mancozeb 75% WP (2.5 g per litre), captan (2 g per litre), myclobutanil (0.4 g per litre), tebuconazole (1 ml per litre), or azoxystrobin (1 ml per litre). Alternate fungicide classes (FRAC codes) to prevent resistance development. Always adhere to pre-harvest intervals and consult your local Krishi Vigyan Kendra or National Research Centre for Grapes (NRCG) advisory.
