# Tomato Two-Spotted Spider Mite Management

The two-spotted spider mite (*Tetranychus urticae*) is a destructive polyphagous acarine pest that feeds heavily on tomato foliage. Thriving under hot, dry conditions (temperatures above 30°C and relative humidity below 50%), spider mite populations can explode exponentially, turning a lush green crop into bronzed, webbing-encased vegetation within 7 to 10 days.

## Identification and Damage Symptoms

Spider mites are microscopic (approximately 0.5 mm), oval, yellowish-green arachnids with two prominent dark spots on either side of their bodies. They inhabit and feed primarily on the undersides of tomato leaves. 
Initial foliar symptoms appear as fine, pale yellow, white, or silvery speckling (stippling) on the upper leaf surface caused by mites piercing individual plant epidermal cells and sucking out the chlorophyll. As mite feeding intensifies, leaves turn chlorotic, bronze, dry, and brittle, and drop off prematurely. 
In moderate to heavy infestations, extensive fine silken webbing blankets the leaf undersides, growing tips, and flower clusters, upon which thousands of tiny moving mites can be seen with the naked eye. Severely infested tomato plants become stunted, experience blossom blast, and produce small, sunscalded fruit.

## Distinguishing Mite Stippling from Fungal Spots and Viruses

Mite damage produces thousands of tiny, microscopic white or yellow puncture dots across the leaf without defined necrotic spot margins. Fungal leaf spots are discrete, larger (2 to 15 mm) lesions with concentric rings or water-soaked borders. Viral infections cause leaf distortion, curling, or patchy mosaic, but completely lack fine silken webbing and puncture stippling.

## Cultural and Agronomic Management

Spider mites flourish on drought-stressed, dusty plants. Avoid water stress: maintain adequate, uniform drip irrigation to keep plants turgid. Keep farm borders and access roads dampened or vegetated to suppress blowing dust, as airborne dust particles coat tomato leaves, desiccate predatory insects, and spur mite reproduction. Avoid broad-spectrum synthetic pyrethroid insecticides, which wipe out natural mite predators (such as phytoseiid predatory mites and minute pirate bugs) and stimulate female spider mite fecundity (hormoligosis).

## Biological Control

Releasing commercial predatory mites (*Phytoseiulus persimilis*, *Neoseiulus californicus*, or *Amblyseius swirskii*) at the very first sign of stippling provides outstanding biological control, particularly in greenhouse or sheltered production. Apply predatory mites at 2 to 5 mites per square metre before silken webbing develops. Entomopathogenic fungi such as *Beauveria bassiana* or *Hirsutella thompsonii* provide effective organic suppression.

## Chemical and Acaricidal Management

When mite numbers exceed economic injury thresholds (typically 5 to 10 mites per leaflet), apply targeted, selective acaricides that spare predatory bugs: spiromesifen 22.9% SC (1 ml per litre), fenpyroximate (1 ml per litre), abamectin (0.5 ml per litre), or bifenazate. In organic production, spray horticultural mineral oils, potassium salts of fatty acids (insecticidal soap), or cold-pressed neem oil (5 ml per litre) under high pressure directed thoroughly at leaf undersides. Alternate acaricide modes of action (IRAC groups) to prevent rapid acaricide resistance. Consult your local Krishi Vigyan Kendra for approved regional acaricide rotations.
