# Tomato Leaf Mold (Passalora fulva) Management

Tomato leaf mold is caused by the fungal pathogen *Passalora fulva* (formerly *Cladosporium fulvum*). It is primarily a major foliar disease of greenhouse, high-tunnel, and sheltered polyhouse tomatoes, but can also severely damage open-field tomatoes in humid, overcast maritime or tropical environments. The disease requires high relative humidity (above 85%) and warm temperatures (20°C to 25°C) to sporulate and spread.

## Identification

Early symptoms develop on the oldest foliage first as pale green to light yellowish, indistinct chlorotic patches on the upper surface of the leaf. Directly beneath these yellow patches on the lower leaf surface, a dense, velvety, olive-green to grayish-brown fungal mold forms. As the fungal spores mature, the mold turns chocolate brown and powdery. Infected leaves curl upward, wither, turn completely brown, and drop prematurely. While the fungus rarely infects tomato fruit directly, extensive defoliation stunts fruit enlargement, severely curtails total yield, and exposes fruit to direct solar radiation and sunscald.

## Distinguishing Leaf Mold from Powdery Mildew and Early Blight

Leaf mold produces velvety, olive-green to chocolate-brown fungal growth strictly on the lower leaf surface corresponding to yellow patches on the upper surface. Powdery mildew (*Leveillula taurica* or *Oidium neolycopersici*) produces white powdery patches primarily on the upper surface. Early blight produces dark brown circular lesions with distinct concentric target rings and dark margins.

## Cultural and Greenhouse Environmental Control

Managing humidity is the primary non-chemical method for preventing leaf mold:
1. Ventilation: Increase greenhouse ventilation by keeping ridge vents and side curtains open, and operate horizontal airflow (HAF) fans continuously to prevent stagnant pockets of humid air.
2. Heat and vent: In protected polyhouses, raise temperatures slightly before dawn and vent the humid air to prevent relative humidity from exceeding 85% and eliminate condensation on leaves.
3. Spacing and pruning: Ensure wide plant spacing (at least 50 cm between plants within rows). Prune lower leaves up to the currently ripening fruit cluster to facilitate unimpeded air circulation beneath the canopy.
4. Drip irrigation: Use exclusively drip irrigation at the root zone; never wet the tomato foliage.

## Resistant Varieties

Plant tomato varieties with *Cf* resistance genes (*Cf-2*, *Cf-4*, *Cf-5*, *Cf-9*, or *Cf-11*). Breeders frequently introduce multi-race *Cf* resistance into modern commercial greenhouse hybrids, offering complete immunity against prevalent regional physiological races of *Passalora fulva*.

## Organic and Chemical Management

In organic production, copper-based fungicides (copper hydroxide at 2 g per litre) or potassium bicarbonate (3 g per litre) can be applied preventatively to leaf undersides before mold colonies establish. 
In conventional production where leaf mold threatens canopy retention, effective fungicides include difenoconazole (0.5 ml per litre), azoxystrobin (1 ml per litre), chlorothalonil (2 g per litre), cyazofamid, or fluxapyroxad. Direct spray nozzles upward to thoroughly coat the undersides of the leaves. Consult your local Krishi Vigyan Kendra for recommended fungicide rotations.
