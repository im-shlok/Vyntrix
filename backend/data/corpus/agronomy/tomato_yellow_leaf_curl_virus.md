# Tomato Leaf Yellow Virus (Tomato Yellow Leaf Curl Virus - TYLCV) Management

Tomato yellow leaf curl virus (TYLCV) is a devastating begomovirus transmitted exclusively by the sweetpotato whitefly (*Bemisia tabaci*). It is one of the most limiting factors for open-field and greenhouse tomato production in tropical and subtropical regions, capable of causing 100% crop loss if infection occurs before flowering.

## Identification

Infected plants exhibit severe upward and inward curling and cupping of leaflet margins, resembling little cups or spoons. Leaves are markedly reduced in size, become thickened, puckered, and display intense interveinal chlorosis and golden-yellow coloration along the margins. The entire plant becomes severely stunted with shortened internodes, giving it a compact, bushy, or "bonsai" appearance. Flowers typically drop off prematurely (flower abortion) before setting fruit; fruit set is virtually zero if infected prior to flowering, while existing fruit remains small, hard, and unmarketable.

## Distinguishing TYLCV from Physiological Leaf Roll

Physiological leaf roll occurs in response to high temperatures, heavy pruning, or excessive soil moisture; the lower leaves roll upward into tubes, but the leaves remain thick, leathery, and dark green without any yellowing or plant stunting. In contrast, TYLCV produces intense golden yellowing, stunted young growth, upward cupping, and complete cessation of flowering.

## Vector Biology and Transmission Dynamics

TYLCV is not transmitted mechanically or through seeds. It is transmitted in a persistent, circulative manner by adult whiteflies (*Bemisia tabaci*, notably the B and Q biotypes). A whitefly requires only 15 to 30 minutes of feeding to acquire the virus, and after an 8- to 24-hour latent period, can transmit the virus to healthy plants for the rest of its 30-day life span.

## Integrated Cultural and Physical Management

1. Plant resistant or tolerant F1 hybrids carrying the *Ty-1*, *Ty-2*, *Ty-3*, or *Ty-5* resistance genes.
2. Nursery protection: Raise tomato seedlings in insect-proof nursery houses enclosed with 40- to 50-mesh nylon netting to keep whiteflies out until transplanting.
3. Yellow sticky traps: Install 15 to 20 yellow sticky traps per acre at canopy height to continuously monitor and mass-trap adult whiteflies.
4. Reflective silver mulches: Use silver or aluminum-coated plastic mulches on planting beds. The reflected ultraviolet light disorients flying whiteflies, reducing crop landings by up to 80% during the critical first 30 days after transplanting.
5. Roguing: Carefully remove and destroy infected plants during the first 3 to 4 weeks after transplanting in sealed plastic bags to eliminate local virus reservoirs.

## Vector Control (Chemical and Organic)

Whitefly vector suppression is the primary chemical target. Apply systemic insecticides at or shortly after transplanting: imidacloprid (0.3 ml per litre) or thiamethoxam 25% WG (0.3 g per litre) as a seedling root dip or drench. Later in the season, rotate to foliar sprays of spiromesifen (1 ml per litre), diafenthiuron (1 g per litre), or pyriproxyfen (1 ml per litre). For organic operations, spray neem oil (5 ml per litre) or insecticidal soap at 5- to 7-day intervals targeting the undersides of young leaves where whitefly nymphs congregate. Consult your local Krishi Vigyan Kendra for approved regional whitefly resistance advisories.
