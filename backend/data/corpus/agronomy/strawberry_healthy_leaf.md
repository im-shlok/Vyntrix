# Healthy Strawberry Leaf Maintenance and Berry Crown Care

A healthy strawberry (*Fragaria x ananassa*) leaf is trifoliolate (composed of three broad, obovate leaflets with coarsely serrated margins), deep vibrant emerald green, glossy on the upper surface, and supported by a long, firm, pubescent petiole arising from the central crown. Intact, healthy foliage drives photosynthetic carbohydrate synthesis necessary for continuous flowering, crown division, and sweet, fragrant berry enlargement.

## Foliage Nutrition and Soil Acidity

Healthy strawberry foliage requires slightly acidic, sandy loam or rich loamy soil with a pH between 5.5 and 6.5. Strawberries are moderately sensitive to soil salinity and chloride toxicity, which produce marginal leaf scorch. Deliver balanced fertility: 60 to 80 kg/ha nitrogen per season, delivered in split applications (avoiding excessive nitrogen during early spring fruiting, which leads to soft berries and vegetative overgrowth). Adequate calcium and boron enhance foliar cell wall integrity and prevent tip-burn on emerging runner leaves.

## Raised Beds and Plastic Mulch Systems

Plant strawberries on raised beds (15 to 25 cm high) covered with black or silver reflective polyethylene mulch. Mulching isolates foliage and fruit from direct contact with wet soil, preventing soil-borne splash pathogens (*Colletotrichum*, *Botrytis cinerea*, *Phytophthora cactorum*). Ensure planting depth is calibrated precisely: the mid-point of the strawberry crown must sit flush with the soil surface. Burying the crown induces foliar rot, while shallow planting desiccates the root system.

## Water and Canopy Microclimate

Strawberries are shallow-rooted with 90% of roots in the top 15 cm of soil. Maintain constant, uniform moisture via dual-line drip irrigation beneath the plastic mulch. Deliver 20 to 30 mm of water weekly. Overhead irrigation should be avoided during flowering and fruiting to prevent foliar leaf scorch and fruit rot.

## Field Scouting and Integrated Pest Management

Regularly examine the undersides of leaves for two-spotted spider mites (*Tetranychus urticae*) and strawberry aphids (*Chaetosiphon fragaefolii*), the primary vector of strawberry viruses. Old, senescing, reddish-brown leaves on the outer perimeter of the crown should be pruned away during routine weeding to improve airflow. Healthy strawberry leaves require no routine synthetic fungicides.
